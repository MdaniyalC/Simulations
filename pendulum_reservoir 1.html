The physics behind it

01

One partner — normal modes beating

Two pendulums linked by a weak spring don't have "a" frequency each anymore — the pair has two shared normal modes, slightly split in frequency. Starting with all the energy in one pendulum is a superposition of those two modes, and they drift out of phase and back into phase on a regular clock. That drift is the energy handoff — perfectly periodic, perfectly reversible.

02

A few partners — quasi-periodic recurrence

With two or three linked partners there are now three or four normal modes instead of two. The motion is a sum of several incommensurate frequencies, so it looks irregular — energy visits each partner in turn — but with only a handful of frequencies, they realign every so often. Poincaré's recurrence theorem guarantees it: a closed, finite system always finds its way arbitrarily close to where it started, eventually.

03

Many, spread-out partners — a reservoir

Add many environment pendulums whose frequencies are spread over a band, and the sum has so many incommensurate frequencies that they interfere destructively for a very long time before ever realigning. The energy doesn't vanish — total energy is still exactly conserved — it just dilutes across so many partners, and the return is delayed so far that it becomes practically unobservable. This is the classical cousin of Fermi's Golden Rule and Wigner–Weisskopf decay: irreversible-looking relaxation emerging from fully reversible, unitary dynamics, once a system talks to a large enough continuum.

04

Infinite partners — provably irreversible

A genuine continuum can be solved exactly: eliminate the bath analytically and the system's own equation of motion becomes a plain damped oscillator, θ̈ + γθ̇ + ω₀²θ = 0. That equation has no coupling term left to bring the energy back — its decay is monotonic by construction, for any amount of time, however long you wait. This preset integrates that limiting equation directly, rather than approximating the continuum with a very large but still-finite N. So unlike ①–③, this isn't recurrence pushed further away: there is no recurrence term at all. It's the classical shadow of real radiative damping and spontaneous emission, where a bound state doesn't survive contact with a true continuum of modes.

The subtlety worth noticing

Set the frequency spread to zero and crank the environment count up anyway — the recurrence never gets pushed out to long times the way it does with a real spread; it stays within a few periods, always. That much doesn't change with N. What does change is subtler: an identical bath collapses onto one collective "bright" mode, but that mode's back-reaction shifts the system's own effective frequency by an amount that grows with N — so the two are increasingly off-resonance, and the exchange gets shallower rather than slower. Either way, what actually manufactures irreversibility (a return delayed indefinitely) is dephasing among distinct frequencies, not sheer numbers. Real environments look irreversible because they offer a genuine continuum of frequencies — the N → ∞, Δω → continuous limit, where the revival time is pushed out to infinity.

Where this whole model comes from

The trick behind every pendulum bath and every photon mode on this page — treat a messy environment as a big collection of independent harmonic oscillators — traces back to Richard Feynman, whose work with F. L. Vernon on dissipative quantum systems became the standard starting point (later formalized by Caldeira and Leggett) for turning "the environment" from a vague hand-wave into something you can actually solve. This page runs that idea twice: once classically, with pendulums; once quantum mechanically, with the two-level atom below.

The model, named

H = H_S + H_B + H_int

Every version on this page splits into exactly these three pieces: a system, a bath, and whatever links them.

H_S

System Hamiltonian — the one thing you actually care about: the swinging pendulum θ₀ above, or the two-level atom |e⟩/|g⟩ below.

H_B = Σᵢ ½(vᵢ² + ωᵢ²θᵢ²)

Bath Hamiltonian — a large collection of independent oscillators (pendulums, or field modes), spanning a range of frequencies ωᵢ, that never talk to each other — only to the system.

J(ω) ∝ Σᵢ κᵢ² δ(ω−ωᵢ)

Spectral density — how strongly the bath couples to the system at each frequency. Its shape is what turns a handful of bounces (Rabi oscillation) into clean, one-way decay. See it live in the spectral density panel above.

The quantum picture — a two-level atom, decaying

Swap the pendulum for a two-level atom — ground state |g⟩, excited state |e⟩ — and the spring for the dipole coupling that lets it emit a photon into the vacuum. Under the rotating-wave approximation, the excited-state amplitude obeys an equation of exactly the same "one thing linearly coupled to many things" shape as the pendulum above — just complex-valued and first order instead of real and second order. It runs on the very same N, spread, and coupling sliders from the apparatus panel: nothing new to tune, just a different physical costume for the same mathematics.

Start the atom in a 50/50 superposition and its Bloch vector begins on the equator — length 1, a fully "quantum" state that can still interfere with itself. As the photon leaks out, the vector both tips toward |g⟩ (it's relaxing) and, for a while, shrinks toward the centre (it's decohering — the field now secretly "knows" whether the photon left, which is exactly the which-path information that destroys interference). Watch the purity readout dip below 100% and climb back up: once relaxation is essentially complete, the outcome stops being in doubt, and the atom ends up in a definite, classical state — |g⟩ — which is why the vector regrows to length 1 at the pole instead of staying shrunk. The dip in between is the wave-to-classical transition; the pole at the end is what "classical" looks like once it's finished.

Before and after the Markov approximation

Before: the exact equation for the excited amplitude has memory — ce(t) depends on its whole past through a memory kernel built from every mode's accumulated phase. With few modes (send N up in the apparatus panel to 1 or 2) this shows up as real physics: Rabi-like oscillation and partial revivals, not decay. Even with many modes, the very first instant is special: since every mode starts empty, ck(0)=0, the population's initial rate of change is exactly zero — decay begins quadratically, not linearly, a signature of the quantum Zeno effect that's easy to miss unless you zoom in on the first few percent.

After: assume the bath is smooth and broadband enough that its memory kernel decays effectively instantly compared to the atom's own dynamics — the Markov approximation — and the memory integral collapses to a single number, the Fermi Golden Rule rate Γ = πκ²⁄(4·Δω/ω₀). The exact equation is then replaced by ċe = −(Γ/2)ce, giving |ce(t)|² = e−Γt: clean, memoryless, monotonic decay — the quantum sibling of preset ④ on the pendulum above, and the starting point for the Lindblad master equations used throughout open quantum systems theory.

Decoherence, specifically: population decay (how much energy is left) and coherence decay (how much of the atom's ability to interfere is left) are related but different things — and this model gets both exactly. Start the atom in a superposition α|e⟩+β|g⟩ instead of pure |e⟩, and the |g,0⟩ branch just sits there untouched (it's a zero-energy eigenstate), while the |e,0⟩ branch decays exactly as before. Tracing out the field gives the reduced coherence ρe,g(t) = αβ*ce(t) — exactly, not approximately, for any N. Since population goes as |ce|² and coherence goes as |ce|, coherence always decays at exactly half the population's rate: T₂ = 2T₁, whenever decoherence has no source but the decay itself. That's the specific, exactly-solvable case shown here — real atoms often decohere faster still, from extra "pure dephasing" this model doesn't include.
